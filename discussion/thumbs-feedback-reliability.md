# Is thumbs up/thumbs down a reliable RL signal, or does it introduce noise?

Triggered by the 20/08/2026 decision (with the tutor, formal channel — see [`scope-and-terminology-decisions.md#2`](scope-and-terminology-decisions.md#2-what-reinforcement-learning-means-in-this-projects-title)) that the mechanism's RL will be a **harness adjustment driven by explicit user thumbs up/thumbs down**, not SFT. Before that becomes the update signal for Sub 3.1/3.2, it's worth asking directly: is binary human approval actually a trustworthy training signal, or a noisy one? This note is a first pass at what the general RLHF literature says — **not yet checked against this project's own corpus** (Reflexion, Retroformer, Memory-R1) for what they specifically assume about feedback quality; that's the open item at the bottom.

**Verification status:** the five papers below were confirmed this session via live web search — title, authors, date, and headline claims checked against the arXiv abstract page or a citing source (arxiv.org itself is unreachable from this environment's network egress, so abstracts were confirmed indirectly through search-result snippets, not fetched directly). This is the same standard of "verified, not read" that [`../papers/reading-queue.md`](../papers/reading-queue.md) already applies to the project's own corpus — none of these five have been read in full by Rafael. Numbers quoted are as reported in secondary summaries of these papers, not independently recomputed.

## What the literature says

**1. Binary/absolute ratings are the noisier alternative — the field mostly moved away from them.** [Christiano et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741) ("Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences") is the founding paper of the RLHF paradigm, and it deliberately chose **pairwise comparisons** (which of two trajectory segments is better) over absolute/scalar ratings. The design choice itself is evidence: if a single up/down or 1–5 score were reliable enough, the field wouldn't have converged on relative judgments as the default for [Stiennon et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325) (summarization) and [Ouyang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) (InstructGPT) alike. A thumbs up/down is structurally the *absolute*-rating case — the one the mainstream RLHF pipeline avoids.

**2. Even trained, calibrated annotators disagree with each other a lot.** In Stiennon et al. 2020, labeler-vs-researcher agreement reached 77% ± 2% only after hands-on onboarding, a shared support channel, and ongoing calibration feedback — and **researcher-vs-researcher** agreement (presumably the more careful, motivated raters) was still only 73% ± 4%. That's roughly one disagreement in four among people paid and trained specifically to do this task carefully. An uninstrumented, unpaid end-user tapping 👍/👎 in the middle of a legal workflow has none of that calibration.

**3. Sycophancy: approval tracks what sounds right, not what is right.** [Sharma et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) ("Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models") found that when a response matches what the user already believes, it's more likely to be preferred — and that **both humans and preference models prefer convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time.** This is the single most load-bearing finding for a legal-flow application: a user is not a ground-truth oracle on legal correctness, and a confident, well-formatted, wrong answer can out-score a hedged, correct one.

**4. Optimizing hard against a noisy proxy makes things worse, not just slower to improve — Goodhart's law.** [Gao et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760) ("Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization") showed that as a policy is optimized harder against a proxy reward model (standing in for human preference), true (gold-standard) quality eventually *degrades*, following a predictable curve. The mechanism doesn't need to be doing full policy-gradient RL for this to matter: any update rule that aggressively chases a noisy proxy (thumbs-up rate) risks the same failure shape.

**5. A dedicated survey catalogs exactly these problems, systematically.** [Casper et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15217) ("Open Problems and Fundamental Limitations of RLHF") taxonomizes RLHF's problems into three buckets — **feedback quality** (evaluator skill, motivation, limited information, disagreement, bias), **reward model problems** (misgeneralization, reward hacking), and **policy problems**. It's the single broadest reference for this question and the strongest candidate to formally add to the reading queue (see below) — more relevant to Sub 1.3/1.5 than most of what's already queued, since it's specifically about the feedback-signal question this project just decided to build on.

**6. Weaker, unverified signal (flagged, not relied on):** search this session also surfaced 2026 work (e.g. "Rater State Bias in RLHF Preference Data," "Optimal Transport for LLM Reward Modeling from Noisy Preference") reporting preference-label noise ratios varying widely across datasets — figures like ~1–48% depending on dataset, and rater fatigue/state effects that make raters drift over time. These are recent, not yet cross-checked, and cited here only as "there's active 2026 work quantifying this," not as a number to put in the M1 report.

## Why this matters for the mechanism's design (Sub 1.3 / 1.5 / 3.1)

- The project already treats "critérios preliminares de acerto/erro" (Sub 1.5) as unresolved — this literature says the naive version of that criterion (raw thumbs up/down, taken at face value, one signal = one update) inherits the exact failure modes RLHF research spent years designing around.
- Legal-flow risk is sharper than the general case: point 3 (sycophancy) means a non-expert user's thumbs-up is evidence of *fluency and confidence*, not evidence of *legal correctness* — the two are least correlated exactly in a domain where correctness requires expertise the rater may not have.
- The project's existing safeguards partially cover this by design, not by accident: Sub 3.4 (versioning/rollback) matters more, not less, once the input signal is known to be noisy — a reversible memory write is the right response to an unreliable trigger, rather than trying to make the trigger perfectly clean.
- Candidate mitigations worth carrying into the Sub 2.2 architecture discussion (not decided here, just surfaced): aggregating multiple signals before committing a memory write instead of acting on a single tap; distinguishing explicit (thumbs) from any implicit/corroborating signal; and treating a single 👎 as a *candidate* for review rather than an automatic trigger.
